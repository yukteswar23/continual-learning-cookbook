Mert Yuksekgonul, Yu Sun and colleagues open with something familiar from any hard homework assignment: “you tried to guess the solution, but these attempts merely produced small signs of life. So you had to take a deep breath and learn from your failed attempts, which made your future attempts more intelligent.” A frozen model searching through prompts “is similar to a student who can never internalize the new ideas behind the assignment” .

10.1A special kind of continual learning

“This form of continual learning is quite special, because its goal is to produce one great solution rather than many good ones on average, and to solve this very problem rather than generalize to other problems.”

Yuksekgonul et al. 2026, Abstract

The episode treats this as its own category of problem . The paper roots it in test-time training, defined (following Sun et al.) as “any kind of training that formulates a potentially different learning problem based on each individual test instance”. The motivation is human: “most human learning happens without a boundary between training and testing. Consider your commute to work this morning. It is both ‘testing’ … and ‘training’” .

10.2Discovery, defined

A problem comes as a text description \(d\), which induces states \(s\) (candidate solutions, such as a GPU kernel) and a continuous reward \(R(s)\) (for example, inverse runtime). Let \(s_\text{sota}\) be the best known solution and \(r_\text{sota} = R(s_\text{sota})\). Then:

Definition (Discovery)“A discovery is an event where a state \(s\) is found such that \(R(s) > r_\text{sota}\). The larger the difference, the more significant the discovery.”

Actions \(a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d, s)\) are thinking tokens followed by code. For maths problems, the code is executed to build a construction: \(s' = \texttt{Python}(\texttt{Parse}(a))\).

Search methods the paper compares against

Best-of-\(N\): independent samples, \(a_i\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d, s)\) with \(s = s_\text{sota}\) or an empty start.

State reuse: warm-start from a buffer of earlier solutions,

\[ s_i\sim\texttt{reuse}(\mathcal{H}_i),\qquad a_i\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d, s_i),\qquad \mathcal{H}_{i+1} = \mathcal{H}_i\cup\{(s'_i, r_i)\}. \]

State-action reuse, also called evolutionary search, as in AlphaEvolve: also feed back context \(c_i\) distilled from earlier attempts,

\[ s_i, c_i\sim\texttt{reuse}(\mathcal{H}_i),\qquad a_i\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d, s_i, c_i). \]

In all three the model’s weights \(\theta\) never change. TTT-Discover changes them.

10.3Why naive RL is the wrong tool

Plain RL at test time maximises expected reward. The paper lists three mismatches with discovery :

  • Objective. “Naive RL optimizes average performance, and is indifferent to the state of the art.” If the record kernel runs in 2000 µs, reaching 1900 µs may need a breakthrough, yet earns almost the same reward.
  • Short horizon. Starting every attempt from scratch limits how far one attempt can go. Reusing solutions extends it.
  • Exploration. Optimizing the average makes the policy collapse “to safe, high-reward actions rather than risky ones that might achieve discovery.”

10.4The two ingredients

An entropic objective that chases the maximum

\[ J_\beta(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\texttt{reuse}(\mathcal{H})}\Big[\log\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[e^{\beta(s)R(s,a)}\big]\Big] \] \[ \nabla_\theta J_\beta(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\texttt{reuse}(\mathcal{H}),\;a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\Big[w_{\beta(s)}(a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\Big],\qquad w_{\beta(s)}(a) = \frac{e^{\beta(s)R(s,a)}}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[e^{\beta(s)R(s,a)}\big]} \]

Advantages are shaped with a KL penalty toward the starting policy:

\[ A(a; s) \;=\; w_{\beta(s)}(a) \;-\; 1 \;-\; \lambda\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(a\mid s)}, \]

The \(-1\) is a baseline, since \(\mathbb{E}[w]=1\). “As \(\beta\to\infty\), the entropic objective tends to the max.” A fixed \(\beta\) was hard to tune across tasks, so \(\beta(s)\) is set adaptively per starting state by limiting the KL of the induced policy. The KL coefficient \(\lambda\) is 0.1 (0.01 for algorithm engineering) .

PUCT to choose where to start

Each state in the buffer is scored as

\[ \text{score}(s) \;=\; Q(s) \;+\; c\cdot P(s)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1+T}}{1+n(s)}, \]
  • \(Q(s)\) is the maximum reward among children of \(s\), not the mean: “we care about the best outcome starting from a state, not the average”.
  • \(P(s)\) grows with \(s\)’s rank by reward.
  • \(n(s)\) counts how often \(s\) or its descendants were expanded.
  • \(T\) is the total number of expansions, and \(c\) the exploration coefficient .

TTT-Discover

Recipe 10.1 · Yuksekgonul et al. 2026

You will need

  • One problem with a continuous, verifiable reward (0 for invalid solutions)
  • An open model (gpt-oss-120b) trainable with LoRA rank 32, via the Tinker API
  • About US$500 of compute: 50 steps × 512 rollouts
  • A buffer of attempts, initialised with an empty or random solution

Method

  1. Pick 8 starting states from the buffer with PUCT.
  2. From each, sample a group of 64 rollouts (thinking plus code) with the current weights. Contexts are capped near 26,000 tokens to leave room for the answer.
  3. Parse or execute each action, check validity, and score its reward. Add everything to the buffer.
  4. Take one gradient step on the whole batch with the adaptive entropic objective, with importance-ratio correction for the sampler/learner mismatch.
  5. Repeat for 50 steps, and return the best state ever found.
Fair comparisonEvery result is compared with Best-of-25,600 using the same model and sampling budget, and with OpenEvolve (an open AlphaEvolve) under the same budget .

Mathematics

Erdős’ minimum overlap problem (1955): split \(\{1,\dots,2n\}\) into two halves \(A\) and \(B\) of size \(n\). Let \(M_k\) count solutions of \(a_i - b_j = k\), and let \(M(n) = \min_{A,B}\max_k M_k\). The goal is to bound \(c = \lim_{n\to\infty} M(n)/n\). Before AlphaEvolve the bounds were \(0.379005 < c < 0.380927\), and AlphaEvolve lowered the upper bound to 0.380924 .

TTT-Discover lowered it to 0.380876, an improvement “16 times larger than AlphaEvolve’s improvement over the previous state-of-the-art”. It did this with a 600-piece asymmetric step function, where AlphaEvolve’s was 95 pieces and symmetric, and the best human result 51 pieces. The algorithm it found uses “FFT-accelerated gradient descent combined with random hill climbing and simulated annealing” .

Both halves are needed. Learning without reuse barely helps, and reuse without learning stalls. “Without any test-time training, both the mean reward and the max reward stagnates” .

The authors’ own limits“The current form of our method can only be applied to problems with continuous rewards, and the most important direction for future work is test-time training for problems with sparse or binary rewards, or problems in non-verifiable domains.”

What to take from this chapter

  1. When the goal is one record-breaking answer, train on the test problem itself. The model’s own attempts are the most relevant data there is.
  2. Optimize for the maximum, not the mean: an entropic objective \(\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\beta R}]\) and PUCT reuse scored by the best child.
  3. Forgetting other skills hardly matters here, which shows how much the right continual learning method depends on the goal.
